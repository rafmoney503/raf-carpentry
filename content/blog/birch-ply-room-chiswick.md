This small room in Chiswick had to work as a bedroom and a study. In a room this size, freestanding furniture eats the floor. Built-in furniture uses the walls instead, right up to the ceiling.

So everything here is fitted, and everything is birch ply.

## What went in

- A tall wardrobe on one side, with hanging space, shelves and drawers
- Cupboards running overhead
- A desk hung on the wall under the window, with a drawer
- A wall lamp over the desk, and a pegboard by the window for the small things

![The whole room from the doorway](/images/blog/birch-ply-room-chiswick/doorway.jpg#narrow)

## Insulate first

The desk sits against the outside wall, so before any furniture went in, that wall was insulated. Insulation boards went in between battens, the joints were taped, and the wall was lined.

It is a step that is easy to skip and hard to add later. Once a wall is covered in furniture, you are not getting behind it again. Doing it first also means everything fixes into something solid.

![Insulation boards between battens on the outside wall](/images/blog/birch-ply-room-chiswick/insulation.jpg) ![Birch ply cut to size on a bench in the room](/images/blog/birch-ply-room-chiswick/bench.jpg)

## Cut and built in the room

I set up a bench in the room and cut the birch ply to size right there. Working in the room means every part can be checked against the actual walls, which are never as square as the drawing.

The walls were lined in ply, then the wardrobe, the overhead cupboards and the desk went in.

![The wardrobe carcass lined in ply](/images/blog/birch-ply-room-chiswick/lined.jpg) ![Overhead cupboards and desk going in](/images/blog/birch-ply-room-chiswick/cupboards.jpg) ![The finished desk under the window](/images/blog/birch-ply-room-chiswick/desk.jpg)

## Why birch ply

Birch ply is strong, stays flat and does not mind being screwed into near its edges. The edges show the layers of the board, which suits a room like this one: it looks like what it is.
